# Realness, and the extremes

*Written at the owner's request during the editorial pass: "consider
REALNESS: most people aren't all good or all evil, they are human.
Consider also the extremes and truly heinous surprises."*

Two halves. The first is an audit of how human the cast is, and it finds
a problem the genre rarely has: **this valley is too forgivable.** The
second drafts the extremes: the truly heinous turns available to this
story, each planted, each fair, each tied to the theme, with the rules
that keep them from turning into cruelty for its own sake. The craft
behind both is in `CRAFT_STUDY.md` §2 (character), §3 (twists) and §5
(where darkness goes hollow).

---

## Part 1. Realness

### 1.1 The diagnosis: every sin has a noble motive

Read the cast's secrets in a row (`STORY_BIBLE.md` §3 and §5):

| Person | The secret | The reason given |
|---|---|---|
| Vonnra | Drowned a dozen travellers to make an Unchained | To save the valley |
| Holloway | Signed for boots that never came; the garrison died barefoot | To cover his commander |
| Harlan | Sells blasting ember to the Dig | To keep the Company alive |
| Pell | Paid bandits to take a caravan | To keep the ember from the Dig (and for profit, "because he cannot help it") |
| Redcowl | Bandit with cages | To feed forty-one mouths |
| Rav | Tipped off his brother | Family; "ashamed of all of it" |
| Rook | Paid by Vonnra for news of the ford road | Turns when she learns what the money bought |
| Ysolde | Sells the names of the risen | To feed her caged brother |
| Sella | Sells pillow talk | It is her trade; she would lie for you |
| Jessop | Sent the wagons off the road | Paid |
| Grimtunnel | Stole the heart | Faith |

Every one of them is a good person who did a bad thing for a good
reason, or a bad thing for an understandable one. Pell comes closest to
a human mix ("and for profit, because he cannot help it"), and even his
greed is in the service of the right outcome.

This is the opposite of the grimdark failure. It is the failure of the
**sympathetic-reason trap**: in reaction to cartoon villains, every
dark act is given a motive the reader can forgive, until the whole town
is made of tragic necessity. It is a gentler world than it looks. Real
people are not like this. They also do terrible things for small
reasons: fear, laziness, spite, lust, envy, money they did not need,
not wanting to be the one to make a fuss, or not thinking about it at
all. And they do kind things for bad reasons. The reader does not
recognise a person in a motive that is always defensible. They recognise
a person in the motive that is *beneath* the defensible one, the one
the character would never say.

The study's best villains have both. Martin's Jaime pushes a child from
a tower to protect his lover *and* his own skin; he saved a city *and*
he enjoys being hated. Hobb's Regal is a tragic hero in his own story
*and* a petty, cruel, vain man. Abercrombie's Glokta is kind to Ardee
for no reason he can name *and* tortures for a living with a craftsman's
pride. Erikson asks of bleak fiction "why did you bother telling me
that?"; the answer is never "because everyone had a good reason".
Martin's guiding line, from Faulkner: "the human heart in conflict with
itself" (`CRAFT_STUDY.md` §2.1). A heart with one good reason is not in
conflict.

### 1.2 The second diagnosis: the good are spotless

The inverse problem: the kind people have no shadow.

- **Rook** is kindness said sideways. Her only fault, the money from
  Vonnra, is innocent ("she pays everyone for something") until she
  learns better in Act 2, and then she turns.
- **Chid** is "the gentlest person who knows" and "the one person who
  never asked anything" of the survivor.
- **Wenna** is "very rude, very kind".
- **Tam** is right, every time.
- **Keegan** is comic, sincere, and the last true knight.
- **Maeca** is wronged, loyal to wolves, quiet.

Tam is nine, and should stay spotless; a child who is right and
unbelieved is a fixed point every dark story needs. Everyone else needs
one thing the player would rather not know. Not a redemption arc: a
fact. The kindest person in the valley should have done the cruellest
thing in it, for the most ordinary reason. That is the most realistic
note a dark fantasy can strike, and it is the note that makes the
reader trust the rest of the book.

### 1.3 The principle: three layers of motive

For every named person, the bible should be able to answer three
questions:

1. **The reason they give.** (What they would say at a trial.)
2. **The reason they believe.** (What they tell themselves at night.)
3. **The reason underneath.** (What a friend who knew them for forty
   years would say, quietly, after the funeral.)

The game has the first two for almost everyone. The third is where the
humanity is, and it is almost always small: vanity, fear, loneliness,
habit, wanting to be thanked, wanting to be left alone, envy of
someone's ease, the money for a pair of boots.

### 1.4 The third layer, person by person (proposals)

Each of these is offered as a fact for the bible. Most are never said;
the bible's own rule holds ("they never say it; they talk around it").
Each lists the seed that already supports it, so that it costs little.

**Vonnra.** *Gives:* the valley. *Believes:* duty, her line's last
service. *Underneath:* **pride.** She is the last of a family that once
kept the Legion's keys, and she cannot bear to be the one in whose
time the chain broke. The valley is real to her; being the binder who
failed is more real. She would rather drown carters than be remembered
as the end of her family. Seed: "I am not angry. I am arranging." (Her
first feeling is about her arrangement, not the danger.) And see the
extremes, 2.3.

**Holloway.** *Gives:* he covered his commander. *Believes:* he had no
choice; a quartermaster signs what he is told. *Underneath:* **he sold
the boots.** The relief wagons were turned back, and the boots that
were on them went somewhere, and the Watch has eaten ever since. "Eleven
men, a year unpaid" is not the whole truth: the Watch has been unpaid by
the north for a year, and has been paid, quietly, for ten years, out of
what Ashford did not get. He is still feeding his men on the garrison's
bare feet. Seed: "I counted boots for the Ashford garrison, once, and I
was good at it." "Never says sorry; the nearest he gets is paying for
something." (He has been paying for something for ten years.)

**Maeca.** *Gives:* nothing; she is the wronged party. *Underneath:*
**at Ashford she finished the wounded.** Three days in a cave mouth
"with the garrison dead round me": not all of them were dead when she
went in. She did what had to be done for the ones who could not be
carried, and she did it to a boy she had been sleeping with. She has
never hunted a wolf because the Pack is the only thing that saw it and
did not look away. Seed: "Men are easier. They keep to paths and they
talk while they walk." "Fed some of them, once. Buried more." "That's
all of why. Don't make it a story."

**Rook.** *Gives:* "she pays everyone for something". *Underneath:*
**she knew.** Not the whole of it, but enough: she watched the carters
go down the south road on toll work and not come back, and she took
double for the survivor and asked nothing, because the Last Lamp was
failing and she was not going to be the woman who lost Ashe's inn. Her
turn in Act 2 is not innocence learning; it is a woman deciding she can
no longer pretend. That is a better arc. Seed: "Don't look like that,
pet." (She has seen that look before, on her own face.) "I'd told her
about you before you'd finished your stew."

**Wenna.** *Gives:* she nursed the fever year. *Underneath:* **she
chose who got the bitterroot.** There was not enough, and she gave it
to the ones she judged would live, and she was right about most of
them, and wrong about Harlan's sister, whom she passed over for a man
she liked better. She has never named the fever year's dead because one
of the names is a decision. Seed: "never names the fever year's dead";
"I make things that stop you dying, and a few that don't"; her "tea for
keeping it and a tea for not". It is also why she is so hard on the
survivor who sells the cure.

**Chid.** *Gives:* he is the gentlest person who knows. *Underneath:*
**he watched.** See the extremes, 2.2. It is the most important single
proposal in this document.

**Keegan.** *Gives:* she is probationary, comic, sincere. *Underneath:*
**she has done it before.** Chapter four is not theory to her. In her
first month as a probationer, in the north, she returned an Unchained
to the dark: a girl of fourteen who had drowned in a mill-race and got
up. She did it by the handbook, with the prayer, and was posted south to
a gate nobody uses, and nobody has answered her letters since. The
lecturer's voice, the litotes, the jokes about chapter eleven are what
is left of the person who did that. The contraction that slips when she
sees the survivor's health is not only a tell that she has seen the
signs; it is the sound of her remembering the mill-race. Seed: "You
would not like it if I saw them." "I've got to go and read something."

**Brannoc.** *Gives:* he did not ask who bought the irons.
*Underneath:* **he knew whose square coin it was, and he took the money
because his daughter needed boots.** See the extremes, 2.1.

**Harlan.** *Gives:* to keep the Company alive. *Underneath:* **he likes
being the man who brings salt to the valley**, and the B.E. paid for
the new sign over Coyle Trading. His grief for Jory is real; his guilt
is real; and he has never once thought of the pipe-lads (Snib: "Pipe-lads
go blind by spring"), because they are lamplings, and they do not count.
Seed: "I don't ask the salt what it's for, friend."

**Pell.** Already the most human of the cast ("and for profit, because
he cannot help it"). Add one ugly true thing: **he did not go up to
collect his sister's books after the Fall out of love; he went to
collect her savings,** and has been ashamed of it for ten years, and has
counted ever since because counting is the only grief he can do. Seed:
"I came up to collect them, after. There wasn't an Ashford to collect
them from."

**Redcowl.** *Gives:* he feeds his prisoners before his own people.
*Underneath:* **and the ones nobody would pay for went in the ravine.**
"A man in a cage is worth something to somebody, and a man in a ditch is
worth nothing to anybody. I've buried enough worth-nothings for one
life." He has also made some. The fourth cage (Ewan, who "did not last")
is not a mystery: Redcowl knows what happened to Ewan. Seed: Jory's "There
was a fourth cage. Was."; the morning report, "There isn't a fourth cage
any more."

**Rav.** *Gives:* ashamed of the tip-off. *Underneath:* **he chose which
prisoners were worth keeping.** He was the Roost's doctor; the sick
ones he did not treat. Seed: "Half my patients die. The other half pay."
(It is a joke. It was also a policy.)

**Sella.** *Gives:* she sells talk; she would lie for you. *Underneath:*
**she sold Jessop.** Jessop's bragging upstairs reached Vonnra through
Sella, and that is how Vonnra knew she had a clerk who would do anything
for silver, and could be sent through a door. She does not know that is
what it bought. If she learns it in Act 2, it is the first thing about
her work she is sorry for. Seed: "He was a Tuesday man. You could set a
clock by Jessop."

**Jory.** *Gives:* he did not know what he carried. *Underneath:* **in
the cage, he gave Ewan up.** When the Kerchiefs wanted to know which of
the four would make trouble, the frightened boy pointed, to save
himself. Ewan was the fourth cage. That is why he shouts the name in his
sleep. Seed: "Jory Coyle woke the inn twice in the night, shouting for a
man called Ewan." "I thought I'd die in that cage."

**The survivor.** See the extremes, 2.5.

**The ones who should stay as they are.** Tam (always right, a child).
Snib (exactly what he looks like; survives everything; the bible says
so and the game needs one creature with no secret). Greymuzzle (an
animal; never a moral agent). Grimtunnel (a believer whose sincerity is
his whole horror). Nell, Wat and Corran (the dead, who are only what is
said of them).

### 1.5 Kindness from the wrong people

Realness runs both ways. The same audit should give the cast's harder
people one unearned kindness each, done for no reason they could
defend:

- **Pell** pays for Aldo's widow's journey up from Low Kiln, anonymously,
  and tells nobody, and charges Holloway for the cart.
- **Redcowl** sends Tam's family a ham in the fever of the breakthrough,
  with no note, because his mother would have.
- **Vonnra** has paid Chid's rent at the shrine for forty years. He has
  no idea.
- **Holloway** kept Corran's name on the roll, unpaid, for a year after
  he wrote him down as a deserter, so his widow could draw his ration.
- **Sallow** (Act 2) has kept Edric alive, fed, warm and read to for
  twelve years, when it would have been cheaper to burn him.

None of these redeem anyone. That is the point. People are not a
ledger, even in a valley that keeps one.

### 1.6 How to show it without saying it

The bible's rule ("they never say it; they talk around it") stays. The
third layer comes out in four ways, in this order of preference:

1. **A detail the player sees** (the new boots; the sign over Coyle
   Trading; Corran's ration).
2. **Someone else says it** (a morning report; a folk line; the dead
   watchman's book).
3. **A slip** under stress (Keegan's contraction; Harlan stopping dead).
4. **Once, said**, in Act 3, by the person, as the one written
   exception to their "never".

## Part 2. The extremes

The study is clear on what makes a heinous turn work rather than merely
shock (`CRAFT_STUDY.md` §3.7, the Red Wedding rules, and §5):

1. **Its cause is a human choice**, ideally a virtuous or ordinary one,
   not a monster's appetite.
2. **The law it breaks was set up as sacred first** (guest right). In
   this valley the sacred things are: the dead lying down; a child being
   believed; the Last Lamp's door; a smith's mark; a promise to a wolf.
3. **The clues are already on the page**, and the last of them arrive
   through the senses just before.
4. **It lands on the theme** (the debt to the dead).
5. **It is said in one plain line and not dwelt on** (`VOICES.md`). The
   horror is in what it recontextualises, not in description.
6. **It is rationed.** One per act at most of the very worst kind.
   Twist inflation kills extremes faster than anything (§3.13).

What follows is ranked by power, with the cost to the bible and the
act where each belongs. Take two or three; not all.

### 2.1 Nell's new boots (Brannoc) — Act 2, the irons

**The turn.** Brannoc knew the square coin was the binders' (the whole
valley knows Vonnra's grandmother's coin; it hangs at her throat). He
took the work anyway, because the irons paid well and Nell was going to
her aunt at Low Kiln and her boots had holes in them. The ten irons that
drowned her bought the new boots she drowned in.

**Why it is fair.** It is planted three times already: the prologue's
"red hair under the weed, and new boots"; Brannoc's own description,
"Twelve. Red hair. New boots."; and "Didn't ask who. ...Should've." Every
player has read "new boots" twice and thought it was colour.

**Why it is human.** He is not a villain. He is a father who did not ask
a question he could guess the answer to, because the money was for his
daughter. It is the most ordinary sin in the world, and it killed her.

**How it comes out.** Not from Brannoc. From Rook, in Act 2, if Brannoc
breaks the last two irons: "He bought her those boots with the ford
money, you know. Paid Kettle the cobbler in square coin. Kettle showed me.
I didn't think, pet. Nobody thought." And then Brannoc's scene, in which
he says nothing at all, and the narrator says he takes the two irons and
the boots' last-maker's form down off the wall, and puts them both in the
fire.

**Cost.** One line of Rook, one scene. It is the cheapest extreme in this
document and the one I would take first.

### 2.2 Chid watched (Chid) — Act 3, Chid's truth

**The turn.** Chid was at the ford, every night the carters crossed. He
saw the blue lights go out one by one (Rook: "Chid came in babbling about
blue lights going out at the crossing"). He knew what the lit lamps meant;
he has known what the Wardens are for two hundred years. He could have
gone to Holloway, or broken the irons, or stood in the road. He did not.
He sat in the reeds and watched the travellers drown, a dozen of them,
Nell and Wat among them, **because he was hoping one of them would get
up.** He has been alone for two hundred years, the last of the Order,
the only one of his kind, and he wanted someone like him so badly that he
let the valley's poor drown for the chance of it. And then one did, and
he pulled the survivor out, and built the fire, and sat with the body
while it began to breathe, and left before they woke because he could
not bear to be thanked.

**Why it is fair.** "It's been a long time since I saw anyone do that.
I'd forgotten how it looks." (He watched for it.) "Can't sleep either?"
(his night bark). Rook's blue lights. "Morning comes. It always has. I
should know." The folk line that he is "a fool, says so himself". The
shrine's flame that "kept the dead lying down": he knows what keeps them
down and what lets them up. And the bible's own sentence: "He has known
all along and wants the survivor to choose without thinking of him; he is
the one person who never asked anything of them." He never asked
anything because he had already taken it.

**Why it is human.** It is the gentlest person in the game doing the
cruellest thing in it for the most human reason there is: loneliness. It
does not make him a villain. It makes him the most real person in the
valley, and it makes every kindness he has shown the survivor both true
and unbearable.

**How it comes out.** In Act 3, in his own words, only if the survivor
asks the right question ("Who built the fire?"), as the one exception
to his voice's "never cynical; never says how old he is":

> (Chid) "I did. ...I'm sorry. I'd like to tell you I was passing. I wasn't passing. I'd been sitting in the reeds every night since the lamps were lit, waiting to see if anyone would get up. Twelve. I counted. You were the twelfth. (He is very quiet, which he never is.) I'm not sorry you got up. I'm sorry about the eleven. I'm sorry I'm not sorrier."

**Cost.** One scene in Act 3; the Act 1 seeds are already there. It
deepens the Act 3 endings: if Chid offers to lie down in the chain in
the survivor's place (ending A's variant), the player now knows what he
is paying for.

### 2.3 Vonnra chose Nell (Vonnra) — Act 3, Vonnra's truth

**The turn.** The carters were not a lottery. Vonnra chose who crossed,
and she chose with a binder's arithmetic: people who would be missed
least, and people whose deaths would bind someone she needed. Brannoc
had seen the coin. A smith who knew whose coin it was could talk. So she
put the toll work in the way of Brannoc's daughter, through Wat, who
owed her (the notice; "paid DOUBLE"; Wat's grey mare), knowing that a
smith with a drowned daughter and a guilty conscience about the irons
would never say whose coin had paid for them. She bound him with his own
child.

**Why it is fair.** "Didn't ask who" (and Vonnra knew he would not ask
twice); "Buyer can come and ask me himself. Or herself." (he suspects);
"I'll know the coin."; the square coin at her throat, which Brannoc
would have seen every time he paid her toll. And Vonnra's voice: she
"never says what she wants" and talks of what is "arranged". "I am not
angry. I am arranging."

**Why it is human, not monstrous.** Vonnra believes the valley lives or
dies by her chain. She has drowned a dozen people already. Choosing *which*
twelve is, to her, simply doing it well. That is the banality of it: not
sadism but administration. Sallow's ledgers and Holloway's lists are the
same instinct at smaller scale. (The bible's own rhyme: everyone in this
valley counts.)

**How it comes out.** In Act 3, and only in her own words if the survivor
accused her in Act 1 (`vonnra.accused`), because only a person who has
been *seen* would confess the detail. Otherwise it is in the pieces on
the stair: a page of her ledger, in violet ink, with names and a column
headed *missed by*. Nell's line reads: *Brannoc. Will not speak after.*

**Cost.** One ledger page; one confession line. It costs the bible
nothing and makes Vonnra the most frightening person in the game, which
she should be, because she is also the most necessary.

**Caution.** Take 2.3 *or* 2.1 as the main Nell turn, not both at full
weight in the same act. Together they are crushing: Brannoc took the
money for the boots, and Vonnra put the boots on the road to bind him.
That is perhaps exactly right for the author's taste. It is too much for
one scene. Put 2.1 in Act 2 and 2.3 in Act 3, and let the second land on
a player who already knows the first.

### 2.4 Holloway sold the boots (Holloway) — Act 2, the boots

See 1.4. **The turn:** the Watch has eaten for ten years on what Ashford
did not get. **The extreme version:** Maeca learns it, and learns too
that the boots she has refused to wear for ten years are on the feet of
the eleven men on the wall, the ones she drinks beside, one of whom is
Aldo, who hummed. If Aldo died at the gate in Act 1 (the wolves),
Holloway buried him in Ashford's boots. Cost: one line of Pell's sister's
letter ("and somebody had signed for them full, and the Watch at the
Waystation has new boots, which I thought funnier still"), and one shot:
Holloway's own boots. Holloway "never says sorry; the nearest he gets is
paying for something". He has been paying for ten years.

### 2.5 The survivor took toll work (the survivor) — Act 2, what you are

**The turn.** The survivor was not a traveller who happened to try the
ford at dusk. They answered the notice. "CARTERS WANTED for the south
road. Toll work, paid DOUBLE for a quick crossing." They took Vonnra's
money, like Wat. They were carrying something for her across the ford
at dusk, and they knew it was dangerous, because "quick means after
dark" and they were paid double for it. The amnesia covers the one thing
the survivor would least like to remember: that they were not unlucky.
They were bought, like everyone in this valley, and they took the coin.

**Why it is fair.** The survivor came "up the Low Ford road" at night,
which only carters did ("Not many, this last year. The ford's been bad
since the winter."). Rook was "paid double for you": Vonnra paid double
for news of one particular carter. Vonnra: "the lamps lit for you."
Holloway: "Either you're very good or very lucky." The far bank: "or you
left it on the far bank. Most do."

**Why it is human.** It removes the survivor's innocence without making
them guilty of anything worse than needing money. They are one of the
valley's debtors. It makes "What will you do with it?" the question
every NPC has already answered.

**Cost.** One item: in the prologue, in the survivor's own pack, a toll
token stamped with three roads, the same as the one trodden into the
bootprint at the vault. Nobody comments on it in Act 1. In Act 2, Keegan
or Vonnra asks to see it.

**Caution.** It touches the player's own character, which players defend
fiercely. Make it optional in tone: the survivor's answer to who they
were (the backgrounds) stays true; this is what they were doing *that
night*. And allow the player to deny it to everyone, including
themselves, as a choice.

### 2.6 Keegan's mill-race (Keegan) — Act 2, chapter four

See 1.4. The comic knight has killed a fourteen-year-old girl by the
handbook. It comes out at the gate in Act 2, when she asks "Did you die
on the Low Ford road?", and the survivor can ask back: "Have you done
this before?" Her answer, the one place she uses no handbook and no
figure of speech: "Once. She thanked me. They do, the first time.
They're frightened." That line makes the duel a tragedy whichever way it
goes. Cost: one dialogue branch.

### 2.7 Jory and Ewan (Jory) — Act 2, Harlan exposed

See 1.4. A seventeen-year-old pointed at another prisoner to save
himself. It changes the Coyle thread from "the uncle lied" to "the whole
family has something to hide, including the boy we rescued". It comes
out to the survivor only, from Redcowl (Act 2, if alive) or Rav, and the
player decides whether Harlan ever learns it. Cost: one line, one choice.
It is a small extreme, which is what makes it real.

### 2.8 Wenna's choice (Wenna) — Act 2, the breakthrough

See 1.4. In the second fever (the breakthrough's bad air), there is again
not enough bitterroot, and Wenna has to choose again, and the player
watches her do it, and this time she chooses Tam's Pa over herself, or
over Harlan, whom she has hated for ten years for a death that was her
own decision. Its power depends on the player not having been told about
the first time: they learn it from what she does. Cost: one scene in an
act the bible already has.

### 2.9 The ones the game should not use

Some extremes are easy and cheap, and they are the ones critics mean when
they call grimdark hollow (`CRAFT_STUDY.md` §5; Liz Bourke on *Prince
of Thorns*). Do not use them as surprises:

- **Sexual violence as a twist.** It is the most common way dark fantasy
  goes hollow, and the romance routes (Sella, Maeca) are built on trust
  and consent. Do not make any of them a trap of that kind.
- **A child's suffering dwelt on.** Nell works because it is one plain
  line and the horror is all recontextualisation. Never more than that.
  (Tam must never be harmed on screen.)
- **"It was all a dream", or a narrator who lied.** Invalidating twists
  (§3.3). The survivor's amnesia may hide; it may not falsify.
- **The kind person was secretly a sadist.** Chid watching (2.2) is
  extreme *because* his reason is loneliness, not cruelty. Make him enjoy
  it and the turn is cheap.
- **Everyone is terrible.** Ration: one major extreme per act, and leave
  a fixed point (Tam, Snib, Greymuzzle, Rook's kitchen) that the story
  never desecrates. The study's common thread: keep one thing sacred.

### 2.10 Where they sit

| Act | Main extreme | Minor | Fixed points that stay clean |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | (none; plant only) Nell, as written | — | Tam; Greymuzzle; the Last Lamp's door |
| 2 | Nell's boots (2.1), or the survivor's toll token (2.5) | Holloway's boots (2.4), Keegan's mill-race (2.6), Jory and Ewan (2.7), Wenna (2.8) | Tam; Snib |
| 3 | Chid watched (2.2) | Vonnra chose Nell (2.3), and the ember is the dead (`THE_EMBER_REVEAL.md`) | Snib; the dead's own wish to rest |

The heaviest pair is Act 3's: the gentlest person in the valley watched
the drownings out of loneliness, and the light the player has burned all
game is the dead he watched drown. Two turns in one act is the most this
story can carry. The author may judge that one of them belongs to a
later game.

## Part 3. A test for every new scene

Before a scene goes in, ask of each person in it:

1. What is the reason they would give?
2. What is the reason they believe?
3. What is the small, human, unsayable reason underneath?
4. What is one thing they do in this scene that their best self would
   not, and one thing their worst self would not?

And of every extreme:

5. Is it a choice, and an ordinary one?
6. What sacred thing does it break, and was that set up?
7. Is it already planted, at least twice?
8. Is it said in one line?
9. Does it land on the debt to the dead?
10. Is it the only one of its weight in this act?
