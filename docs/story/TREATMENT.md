# Story treatment: Survivor Unchained, Acts 1 to 3

The writer's treatment for the owner's choice, 5 October 2026. It answers the
owner's brief and the editor's letter (`EDITORIAL_LETTER.md`), and it rests
on the beat-by-beat diagnosis (`EMOTIONAL_PASS.md`). Nothing here is in the
game's data yet. When the owner says yes (section 10 lists the choices), the
lines get rewritten, Act 1 first.

**The owner, in his words:**
- "we really want a powerful epic story with real emotional happenings.
  people being devastated, elated, or both. shocks and twists, stuff we
  figured out that was obvious. the whole thing. its all very simple right
  now."
- "twists should either twist a knife in or just make you feel gidd with a
  what the heck just happened."
- "when maeca kills halloway it should be a HOLY SHIT WHAT THE FUCK kinda
  thing."
- Brannoc "needs to hit HARD. so hard."

**What stays.** The world, the spine and nearly every line of prose: the
Morrow, ember as the held dead, the chain, Vonnra's twenty-six, the survivor
risen at the Low Ford, Chid, the Order, the Pack, Redcowl's camp, the
romances, the three endings. The treatment changes the *architecture of
feeling*: where secrets are hidden, where they land, who pays, and what the
player is made to believe before it turns.

---

## 1. The shape in one page

Every act has one devastation the player cannot miss, one twist that
re-reads what came before, one rise of joy, and a laugh in its darkest
stretch. Choices bend how each lands, never whether.

| | Act 1, The Waystation | Act 2, The North Road | Act 3, The Morrow |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Devastation (on the trunk)** | Brannoc learns, in front of her, that the light he made for his daughter is what drowned her | Maeca kills Holloway at the top of a rope, the morning the count came right | Her mother, found and lost again; Chid at dawn asking "Is it morning?" |
| **Twist** | **"You did this."** Her first victory, and her mercy, broke the chain (§3.1) | **"Ninety-two."** Maeca was on the ladder (§4); then **the woman with the lamp**: she cut down her own mother in the ford (§3.3) | **The voice.** The narrator, all along, was her mother (§3.4) |
| **Elation** | Jory home: Harlan's "Alive. Alive!", the town out in the street, Holloway on the gate laughing | The war at the north gate: the line breaking, then everyone she helped arriving, each from a choice made days before | "Lamp's lit, Wick": she was never alone. A two-hundred-year-old priest feeling his knees ache |
| **Laugh in the dark** | Chid singing Nell down flat, and smiling at his own crack | Tam's Pa swearing at his rescuers, and Holloway laughing back up the hole, a breath before the rope | Snib on the stair ("Snib is not going down there. ...Snib is going down there.") |
| **What it costs her** | Her pride in the prologue; a smith's hands; the town's clock | A friend; her trust in a lover or a friend; what she is; her mother's body, by her own hand | Her mother's voice, her own life or her light, and the power she used all game |

**The escalation.** Act 1 is one girl and one father: personal, and the
world only trembles. Act 2 is a farm, a friend, a war, and what she is: the
town's losses and her own nature start costing the same people, because the
hole that kills Holloway is one her own first victory opened. Act 3 is two
thousand years of the valley's dead, and the one voice among them she has
heard every night.

**The thread under it all: a lamp lit for someone.** Everyone in this valley
is keeping a light for somebody. Brannoc lit the ford road so his girl could
walk south safe. Holloway counts the town in at the gate and will not bar it
till the count is right. The survivor's mother stood on the north bank with
a lamp every evening so her child would see her. Vonnra keeps one lamp
burning in her tower, for her arrangement. Every one of those lights is what
kills, or what saves, and in the end the light is the dead.

## 2. The mother: one thing the player can lose

The editor is right (letter §3.9): you cannot be devastated by losing someone
you were never given. So the player is given one specific, slightly odd
thing in the first five minutes, and the dawns take it away a piece at a time.

**She called the survivor "Wick".** In the valley's old speech "wick" means
alive (Rook: "Square's wick with Watchmen this morning"), and it is the
thread a lamp burns on. At dusk, when the survivor was small and out in the
yard, her mother would put the lamp in the window and call from the door:
**"Lamp's lit, Wick. Stay where it reaches."** It is the Order's evening call,
the one the Waystation's gate says every dusk without knowing whose it was,
made small and kept in one family. The call is the valley's; the name is
hers.

- **C04, the first dawn** (trunk): *"You try to call up your mother's face,
  and find it is not quite where you left it. Her voice is still there. 'Lamp's
  lit, Wick. Stay where it reaches.'"*
- **The first dusk at the gate:** the Watch call the lamps, and she turns her
  head. *"You have heard that said at a door."*
- **The dawns take it.** One line now and then, at the night's end:
  - day 3, *"Her voice is going the way her face went. You still have the
    word."*;
  - after Act 2's turn, *"Somebody used to call you something at dusk. One
    short word. It was yours."*;
  - and then the line stops coming. The player notices what is gone because
    the game stops saying it.
- **The bottom of the stair:** she says it (§3.4).

"Wick" can be recorded, where a typed name cannot, so the summit of the game
is voiced. It is the same for a daughter or a son. **One clash to clear:**
combat's lampling swarmer is called "Wick" and its miniboss "The Wick-Mother"
(`Enemies.cs`). I recommend renaming them "Stub" and "The Stub-Mother" (they
wear candle-stubs; it's a display name only).

## 3. The twists

Each passes the editor's six tests (letter §3.7): the false belief, at least
five re-reads, fair but hidden, "it was obvious", a real cost, and the
player's own hands. They are separable: the owner can take any of them.

### 3.1 Act 1: "You did this"

**The false belief.** The prologue was the player's finest hour. They woke
dead, cut their way to the ford, beat the Warden, and gave the thing that
drowned them a mercy. They believe they saved the crossing.

**The truth.** The Warden was the keeper of a link of the chain. Beaten, he
would have held his lamp out of the water until the river ran dry, and risen
again at dusk; his heart would have stayed in the ford. Her "yes" let him let
go. The lamp went into the water, the heart came up, and Grimtunnel was
waiting. Every tremor since, the Dig's new speed, the knocking under Tam's
farm, began the night she walked up the road.

**The nod becomes the game's first choice.** In C03, "Is it morning?" offers
**(Nod.)** or **(Say nothing.)**. Either way he lets go: an old man who has
waited two hundred years does not wait for an answer twice. The knife quotes
whichever she chose.

**The payoff, in full view: the fortune (C09).** The fortune is cut to the
three readings that mattered in this player's game (the editor's note), and
then Vonnra turns the cup over.
- **Nodded:** "He would have held it until the river ran dry. You told him
  it was morning."
- **Silent:** "You did not even tell him it was morning. He let go anyway."
- Then: "The ground has turned over every night since. The Dig digs faster.
  The Penhale boy's floor knocks. ...You have done a great deal of good this
  week, traveller. You also did this."
- The tremor stops her mid-sentence, as it does now. A player who accused her
  can answer, "You lit the lamps." She: "I did not kill him."

**The stinger, readable.** Alone, she opens the ledger, and the camera lets
the player read it: twenty-six lines struck through; at twenty-five, *Nell,
the smith's girl. With Wat.*; at twenty-six, *A woman with a lamp. North
bank. Seventh evening.*; and twenty-seven, not struck: *From the ford. Got
up.* Then Act 2 opens with the knocking under the farm stopping.

**The re-reads.** C03's groan under the ground and every lamp on the road
dipping at once; Grimtunnel's "Downstairs'll be ever so grateful"; Vonnra's
"I am not angry. I am arranging." (`cb_core_stolen`); Holloway's "Don't tell
me why you let it. I've a feeling I'd not like either answer."; Tam's new
line, "It started the night the ford went dark"; every tremor.

**The cost.** The player's pride in the one heroic act they were sure of,
and, in Act 2, a friend who dies in the hole it opened.

### 3.2 Act 2: "Ninety-two"

The twist inside the centrepiece. Section 4 builds it in full.

**The false belief.** Maeca was at the cave mouths when Ashford fell. She is
Holloway's friend: the one who walks him home. If anyone is coming for him,
it is Redcowl.

**The truth.** She was on the ladder, the next hand up, when he dropped the
lid. She was number ninety-two.

### 3.3 Act 2's end: the woman with the lamp

**The false belief.** The drowned in the prologue were strangers, and the
player has already learned their lesson: one of them was Nell. That wound is
Brannoc's, and it has been paid.

**The truth.** The survivor's mother did not die in her bed. When she knew
she was failing she came down to the Waystation to be near the ford road, and
took Rook's back room. Her hands were bad, so Rook wrote her last letter for
her: *Come home by the Low Ford. I'll be at the water with a lamp, so you'll
see me.* (That is the letter in the survivor's coat in C01, its ink run to
blue water.) Rook, paid by Vonnra to report the road, reported that a
traveller was expected on it, and Vonnra paid her double. Every evening the
mother went down to the north bank with a lamp. On the seventh she went down
to the water's edge, and the Warden took her. She is the twenty-sixth line in
the ledger. She rose with the drowned, and her body went and stood on the far
bank, facing up the road her child would come by, holding a lamp that had
gone out.

On the first night, where the road meets the ford, the last of the drowned
stood between the survivor and the water: a woman, swaying, lifting a dead
lamp toward her face the way the Warden lifts his. She did not strike.
**The player cut her down to reach the water.** Every player: it is the
prologue's path, just before C02.

**The plants, all on the trunk, each innocent:**
- C01: the letter whose ink has run.
- The prologue: the drowned woman at the water's edge, who lifts a lamp
  instead of her hands. The narrator says nothing.
- C04, the dawn crossing: *"On the stones at the water's edge, a lamp
  someone has dropped. It has gone out."*
- C04 Part B: Rook at her door looks at the survivor's face one beat too long
  (it is her mother's face), then at the tower.
- Rook's welcome: "Back room's free. Has been a week." The survivor sleeps
  every night in her mother's last bed.
- Day 2's morning report: Chid brought a woman in from the ford before light
  and buried her behind the shrine. "Nobody's claimed her."
- C08: at Nell's burial, a plain marker a few stones along, two days old. Rook
  steps back from Nell's grave and turns away, toward it.
- C09's stinger: line twenty-six, readable.

**The payoff: Act 2's last scene (C32, before "Come down with me").** Rook's
kitchen, the night after the war at the gate. Rook, who never says sorry and
never cries in front of anyone, does neither:
- "She had the back room. Paid in a ring. Her hands were bad, so I wrote her
  letter. 'I'll be at the water with a lamp.' ...I told Vonnra you'd be on
  the road. She paid me double. She's never paid me double for anything."
- "She went down every evening. I watched her go. ...She was your mam, pet."
- **The insert:** the prologue's water's edge, the drowned woman lifting the
  dead lamp, and the survivor's blow, in her own calling's weapon: the
  player's own strike, seen again.
- The narrator, who has never once failed to speak, says nothing at all.
- Then Vonnra is at the kitchen door: "Come down with me. It is not far. It
  is only deep." The survivor goes down the stair with the woman who drowned
  her mother.

**In Act 3** Vonnra reads the ledger aloud (C41), and at twenty-six she reads
"A woman with a lamp" in exactly the voice she used for the others. The
player knows; she does not. The survivor can tell her. Vonnra, after a long
time: "...She was waiting for someone. I wrote that down." She does not say
the thing both of them are thinking: that it is not often the second thing
happens, and that this once, someone down there would not let go. The game
still never says it as a law.

**The cost.** The player's own hands, on the person they came home for.

### 3.4 Act 3: the voice

**The false belief.** The narrator is the game's voice: unplaced, nobody,
furniture. Players do not ask who a narrator is.

**The truth.** When the survivor drowned, her light went down into the
Morrow and came back up muddled, as every risen light does, with one other.
Her mother's, a week down, who knew her and would not let go. Her mother has
been in her light ever since, and **the voice that has told the player
everything, "You get up.", is her mother's.** She says what happens and what
can be seen, never what it means and never what she feels, because an
Unchained who remembers too much stops: she would have sat down in the road
to wait for her.

**What it needs: the narrator is recast as a woman of about sixty**, low,
warm and unhurried, a winter's tale told to a child. Nothing is recorded yet,
so it costs nothing to change. The survivor is never heard in a cinematic, so
no player can match the accents.

**The plants (all already on the trunk, or one line from it):**
1. **"Your bedroll has not been slept in."** The game's first words. A mother
   checking a bed.
2. **"Prints in the frost, your own. They come up from the river. None go
   down to it."** She watched her child walk out of the water.
3. **C04's dawn:** "...not quite where you left it. Her voice is still
   there." The narrator quotes the mother in the narrator's own voice. Of
   course she does: it is hers.
4. **Every rise: "You get up. It takes less than it did."** A mother getting
   a child up.
5. **The narrator never enters a bedroom.** At every love scene the voice
   stops at the door; the scene's prose plays as unvoiced text, and the
   picture holds its one object. Players read it as a rating choice.
6. **The dawn lines that take "Wick" away.** She is narrating her child
   forgetting her, plainly, with no weight, night after night.
7. **The silences:** at the water's edge in the prologue; through Rook's
   confession. The only two places in the game the narrator does not speak.
8. **Act 2's memory swaps:** *"Your father's hands are gone. In their place:
   a hammer, and someone saying 'Mine.'"* She is telling her child that the
   child has lost the father, her husband.
9. **"Somewhere up the street, someone is baking."** The one warm line in the
   prologue. Who notices that, on the worst morning of your life?

**The payoff: the bottom of the stair (C43).** The names; Aldo, Ewan, Corran,
Dannet; a girl saying "Da" once, as an echo (Nell, not pressed again). Then,
from out of all of it, and for the first time from a *place* (the sound
designer moves the narrator's voice out of the air and into the pale thing
below): **"Lamp's lit, Wick."** The voice the player has heard every night of
the game. Choices: "Mam?" / (Listen.) / "Let them go." Then the narrator
speaks in the first person, once:
- "I came up with you. Somebody had to tell you where you were."
- And, if she asks why she never said: "You'd have sat down in the road and
  waited for me."
- Her face is exactly where she left it.

**The endings each settle the voice.**
- **A, re-forge, the survivor lies down:** *"You lie down."* Then, for the
  first time, a second line after it: *"I'm here."*
- **B, break the chain:** the dead go, one by one, her last. Her last line,
  *"It's morning, Wick. Go on."* The epilogue's pages are silent text. The
  game is never narrated again.
- **C, take the light:** two thousand years of voices come up into her, and
  the narrator's grows fainter under them until the last narrated line is
  somebody else's.

**The cost.** The player's belief that they were alone, and every night's
ember, which (Rule 5) crowded her out. "It always takes the names first.
Then the faces." The player did that with their own hands.

### 3.5 Considered, and set aside

- **The Order drowned its own keepers, and Chid drowned Tobin.** A strong
  knife into the gentlest man, re-reading the hymn and *Not Yet*. Set aside
  because Chid works best as irony (the editor's note), and because Act 3
  already asks the player to lose him at dawn. Kept in reserve if the owner
  wants Act 3 darker.
- **The narrator is Brother Tobin.** Giddier, but colder, and the timeline
  fights it: the narrator speaks before the Warden dies.
- **Vonnra is the survivor's aunt.** Soap.

## 4. The centrepiece: Maeca kills Holloway

### 4.1 Holloway, remade

**What he did.** Ten years ago Holloway was the Ashford garrison's
quartermaster: thirty-five, steady, the man who knew every soldier's boot
size and every child's name in the upper town. The night the lower town went
into the ground, half the garrison held the cave mouths on the hillside,
where the old workings come out, and the other half went down the main shaft
on the ladders to bring up whoever was alive. Holloway held the shaft-head:
the windlass, the lamp, and the iron lid the miners drop on a working that
floods or burns. He counted them up off the ladder, aloud, one at a time,
with the lamp in his eyes.

At ninety-one he looked down. Under the last of the living, the dead were
climbing: the ones who had died in the dark down there, getting up the way
the dead do near ember. Behind him, four hundred people in the upper town,
asleep or praying, and their children. He had the time it takes to breathe
in. He dropped the lid and barred it, with the rest of the shaft party and
the townsfolk they were carrying on the ladder under it. He sat on it. They
knocked for three days. On the fourth it stopped.

He wrote the shaft party down with the others, as lost at the cave mouths.
The Watch pays a widow for a man lost on duty; it pays nothing for a man shut
in by his own quartermaster, and it would have hanged the quartermaster, and
then nobody would have been paid. The Watch made him a captain for holding
the upper town. He has paid the widows' shortfall out of his own pay ever
since.

**The editor's test** (letter §3.6, test 2): could the player have made the
same call, for the same reasons? Yes. That is the horror of it.

**Who he is now.** A drunk who counts. He drinks the cheapest thing at the
Flagon and pays for it. He drinks to stop hearing knocking. Every night he
counts the Waystation in at the gate and will not bar it until the count is
right ("Captain won't bar the gate till he's counted the cat," says the town,
and the fox has had the hens twice). Sober, he talks as he does now: clipped
lists, prices, "proof". **Drunk, he talks like his own ledger:** no subjects,
numbers without nouns, entries. "Ninety-one up. Lid down. Three days." That
is his new syntax, and nobody else's.

**He never says sorry.** He dies without saying it. After his death the
survivor finds it written once, in his daybook (§4.6).

**The irony nobody knows:** the Kerchiefs are the upper town. Redcowl's
forty-one mouths, his bairns, are alive because Holloway shut the lid.
Redcowl hates the Watch for writing Ashford off and going home.

### 4.2 Maeca, remade

**Just Maeca.** No "Barefoot" anywhere (the editor's list, letter §4). She
wears boots like everyone, and takes them off only at the Blind.

**What happened to her.** At twenty-three she was the garrison's tracker,
posted that night to the cave mouths. She left her post and went down the
shaft after the boy she had been sleeping with. Holloway, counting faces
with the lamp in his eyes, never saw her go down. She was on the ladder, the
next hand up, number ninety-two, with her face turned up into the shaft-head
lamp, and she saw his face as the lid came down. Then three days in the dark
below, with the dead getting up and the wounded dying. She finished each of
the wounded before they could die and rise. On the third day she followed
the air up an old wolf-run, dragging the last of them, the boy. At the cave
mouth he was dying, and she finished him there before he could get up, with
the Pack's eyes on her from the dark; and an old grey wolf lay down across
the way in and kept the cold off. Three days on wet stone ruined her feet:
two toes gone, the soles scarred white. That is why nobody has held them.

She came to the Waystation a year later, because he was there. She tells
anyone who asks about Ashford three words: "The cave mouths." **It was her
post, and it is his report's lie.** He hears it and believes she held the
hillside that night and lived; he does not know anyone came out from under
the lid.

**What she wants** is the thing she has never been able to name. She has not
decided, in ten years, whether she is keeping him alive or keeping him. She
walks him home when he is drunk. She knows how he takes his drink. She never
lets him pay. She sits up with him at the gate at night with her crossbow
across her knees. Every kindness is true, and every kindness is a hunter
waiting. The rope, at the farm, decides it for her.

**Her signature,** freed from boots: "I track for the Watch, before you ask.
Not wolves." (He pays her out of his own pocket. She takes his money every
week.)

### 4.3 Act 1: making the player love him

Four scenes, every one on the trunk, each costing him something.

1. **The first morning** (C04 Part B). He has been on the gate all night.
   She comes up the road soaked to the skin. He takes off his coat, puts it
   round her shoulders without a word, and walks off. The guard to his mate:
   "That's his only coat." (A note for the cinematics lead's status page:
   this adds a beat to C04's B2.)
2. **The first night fight's return.** Dawn at the south gate, which has
   stood open all night. Holloway asleep on a stool, his lamp burned out, a
   bottle at his boot. Beside him Maeca, awake, her crossbow across her
   knees. She nods at him: "Waits up for you." (A beat.) "Somebody waits up
   for him." He wakes: "One in. Count's right." He bars the gate behind her.
   *(The plant every player sees: the woman with the loaded crossbow sitting
   over him in the dark, read as care.)*
3. **Standing in the gate.** Day 3 or 4, after a night fight. Folk at the
   gate won't let her in: she comes back from the dark every morning as if
   nothing touched her, and "Rook's got a word for that." Holloway, drunk,
   stands in the gateway. "She's in my count." (The crowd.) "Anybody wants
   her out of it comes through me. ...And I'm drunk, so it'll take you all
   morning." It costs him: his own two men stand back, and he stands there
   alone till they go.
4. **The roll.** A night from day 3 on, at the gate, drunk: he recites names
   to the dark, in order, each with a number. "Abbot, nines. Ancell, tens,
   wide. Bede, eights." If she asks: "Garrison. Boot sizes." "I counted boots
   for them, once. Every pair." (The old boots line, kept, now as love.) The
   player sees what he drinks to forget before they learn what it is.

And the laughs: his want ("A posting with walls somebody else has to count.
A dog. Eight hours' sleep in one go. In that order. ...Don't tell the men
about the dog."), kept; and on the morning Jory comes home, Holloway on the
gate: "Three in! Count's— hell, I don't know what the count is!"

### 4.4 Act 1's last nights: understanding the grievance, and the decoy

**The confession** (trunk: the night of Act 1's last tremor, before the
fortune). The ground has turned over. At the gate, very drunk, he hears
knocking that is not there.
- "You ever hear knocking. From under."
- Then, in his ledger syntax, the whole of it: the shaft, the ladder, the
  count, the dead under the last of them, four hundred behind him, the lid,
  three days, the cave mouths, the widows.
- **The player's choices:**
  - "You saved the upper town." He: "Both numbers are right. That's the
    trouble with counting."
  - "You shut them in." He: "I did."
  - "Did anyone come out from under it?" He: "No." (He believes it.)
  - (Say nothing.) He goes back to counting.
- The player now understands what anyone below would want, and may half
  agree. They do not know anyone was below.

**After it, a choice the player can make: tell Maeca.** She was garrison;
she has a right to know. If told, she looks at the player a long time, and
says: "...Did he." Nothing else. (On the re-read, the coldest line in the
game.)

**The decoy is loud, and red.** Redcowl has hated the Watch since the player
met him ("what's left when a town goes into the ground and the Watch writes
it down and goes home"). After the confession a Kerchief bark at the Flagon
says the Roost "has a rope with the captain's name on it". Pell, who went up
to Ashford for his sister's savings and found the lid barred from the top,
lets the player know he is a careful man who knows who. Every threat to
Holloway the player can see comes from red, or from a ledger. None comes from
the woman who walks him home. (If Redcowl died in Act 1, Pell carries the
decoy alone.)

### 4.5 The kill: C20, the Ground Opens (the top of Act 2)

The night after the fortune, the knocking under the Penhale farm stops.
Then the floor isn't there. The barn and the back of the house go into the
Morrow's outer workings, with the two Watchmen Holloway posted there after
Tam's knocking, and Tam's Pa. (If the survivor brought the family into town
the evening before, Tam meets her at the gate: "Pa went back for the dog.")
It is Ashford, exactly, and the player knows it.

1. **The lip.** The town with lamps. A rope round a post. Holloway at the
   edge, drunk, white, shaking: the player has heard why. The survivor can
   go first; he stops her. "No. ...No. I'm going." He goes down first, drunk
   and shaking.
2. **The hole: a story night, played.** Holloway at the rope's foot with a
   lamp, counting them up between waves: "One. Two." The survivor holds the
   dark. Tam's Pa under a beam; she carries him to the rope. Pale segmented
   things coming up out of the workings. Maeca at the lip with her crossbow
   (and the Pack, if it lives) holding the top.
3. **The last.** Everyone up but the two of them. "Up. Go on. I'm last.
   Count's not right till you're up." She climbs. At the lip, Maeca's hand
   takes hers and pulls her over. (If she is the survivor's lover, it is the
   hand she has held.)
4. **The laugh.** Grey light coming. Tam's Pa, on his back in the mud,
   swearing at everyone, at Holloway down the hole: "And you took your bloody
   time, Captain!" Below, Holloway laughs: the first time the player has ever
   heard him laugh.
5. **The climb.** Hand over hand, the lamp hooked at his belt. Maeca at the
   post where the rope is tied, the survivor beside her. His face comes up
   into the light at the lip, under them. He looks up at Maeca and grins:
   **"Count's right."**
6. **The cut.** One stroke of her knife through the rope at the post. The
   camera does not cut away. The rope runs. He does not cry out. The lamp at
   his belt goes down the dark, very small, and goes out.
7. **The silence.** No music. She puts the knife away. To nobody, flat:
   **"Ninety-two."**

Only then do the choices come.

### 4.6 The aftermath, unflinching

- **The lip.** The survivor: "Maeca." / take hold of her / (Say nothing.). If
  she is the survivor's lover and the survivor says her name, Maeca looks at
  her: "You were up." (She waited until the survivor was safe. That is the
  most frightening kindness in the game.)
- **The Pack** (if it lives): Greymuzzle gets up, and walks into the trees,
  and the Pack goes after him. The one thing that saw her at Ashford and did
  not look away, looks away. She stands alone at the lip with the rope's end.
- **The count.** The Watch's ten come running. The youngest, Tolley, the
  guard who said "Dawn arrivals" on her first morning, counts them, because
  somebody has to: "...Ten."
- **What the town does** is the player's to bend: hold her for the Watch (and
  then the gallows, which the survivor can stop or not); let her walk up the
  road and out of the east gate; or stand between her and the Watch, as he
  stood in the gate for the survivor.
- **That dusk.** Someone is standing at the south gate in the last light,
  where he always stood, and his lips are moving. The Watch bar the gate,
  which he never did. The survivor goes out. He does not lift his hands until
  she is close; then he comes at her, because that is what the dead do. She
  lays him down in the road outside his own gate. The gate opens. Whoever
  opens it says "One in": Maeca, if she is free; Tolley, if not.
  *(This beat is the owner's to keep or cut: it is unflinching, and it may be
  one blow too many. I would keep it: it is the act's turn, what she is, in
  one picture.)*
- **His table** (C22, reworked). In the Watch-house the survivor finds
  Sallow's letter, *deliver the one from the ford and the Watch's arrears
  are paid in full*, and under it his answer, written and never sent: "No."
  Dead, he is still protecting her. And his garrison daybook, its last entry
  ten years old, *91 up. Lid down.*; under it, in a drunk's newer hand, every
  name below the lid, with boot sizes; and at the foot, once, the word he
  never said. "Sorry." She can take the book to Maeca, give it to Redcowl, or
  burn it.
- **Redcowl** (C26), if he reads it: "So that's the man kept my bairns
  alive. And I robbed his road for ten years. ...Oh, Mam."
- **Maeca's ends** still branch (leaves with the Pack, if they will have her;
  takes the Watch; dies at the edge), and the most haunting is the one the
  bible already holds: at the war at the gate she wears his coat and counts
  them in.

### 4.7 The editor's seven tests

1. **The player loves him:** four trunk scenes in Act 1, each costing him,
   one drunk and failing (§4.3); he laughs, and makes the player laugh.
2. **A good man's decision:** a breath to choose between the ladder and four
   hundred children. The player would have done it.
3. **Planted but hidden:** no Barefoot; no grievance; she is his kindest
   friend; the threat is dressed in red.
4. **In front of the player, fast, at the worst moment:** one knife stroke,
   no speech before, at the moment of his redemption, a breath after his
   first laugh.
5. **Inevitable afterwards:** the crossbow across her knees at the gate;
   "The cave mouths."; "Men are easier. They keep to paths and they talk
   while they walk."; "Go on. Holloway'll count us."; "I've lain with my ear
   to a lot of things to see if they'd live."; "...Did he."; she never let
   him pay; she pulled the survivor up first.
6. **Not optional:** a fixed point. The player bends what follows.
7. **Worse if she is the lover:** the same scene; the hand that pulled her up
   cut his rope; "You were up."

## 5. Brannoc: he made it, and he knows it in front of her

**The irony is set on day one, out loud.** The player has fought the drowned
in the ditch, and read *"The one with the reins was a girl, twelve at most:
red hair under the weed, and new boots."* Then they meet the smith.

**His pride (trunk: his first meeting and his barks).** Brannoc's never is
small talk. The one thing he talks about unasked is his girl, and it is the
only time he sounds happy.
- First meeting, as she turns to go: "Low Ford road, you came up? (The
  hammer.) Lit that. Ten irons. Best I've done."
- Barks at the forge, days 1 and 2: "Girl's first new boots. Irons paid for
  'em." / "Told her: walk where it's lit. Nothing on that road you can't
  see."
- A folk line gives Wat his life first: "Wat's grey mare won't take a bit in
  winter. He warms it in his armpit." (Act 2's memory swap pays it.)

The player now carries a bomb under every visit to the forge.

**C07, the question, now on the trunk.** From day 2, the first time she
passes the forge by day, he asks; she does not have to come to him. Every
word of it stays: the phrases between strokes, "Mine. Nell.", the iron going
grey. Then, on the truth, **it goes on**. Her lamp-iron is at her belt, where
she always carries it (section 9). His eyes go to it. (Without it, he asks
what the thing in the water held, and she tells him.)
- "That was in its hand. The thing in the water."
- She can say "Yes." He holds out his hand. She gives it him. His thumb finds
  the mark under the socket.
- "Lifts it up. To see your face. ...Does it."
- "It does."
- (A long time. The forge ticks.) "Knew my mark before she knew her letters."
- (Longer.) **"She'd have thought I'd come for her."**
- Then, because he needs one mercy: "...Was it quick?" "It was quick." "...Thank
  you." Or "It wasn't." He nods, once, as at a price.
- "Forge is shut."

This is the longest thing he says in the game, and it is not about himself.
The scene stops for it: no music, the forge ticking, the camera on his thumb
on the mark. On the "gone" answer ("The water took her") it plays the same,
without "Was it quick?": water's quick.

**C14, the road back: one action, held.** "You know the place." If she goes
with him, they walk down the road between his irons and he does not look up,
until the last iron, over the ditch. He stops under it. He looks up at it,
once, and reads his own bracket. Then he reaches up and closes his bare hand
round the light until it goes out. The hiss, and smoke between his fingers.
He makes no sound. He opens his hand. Then he gets down into the ditch for
her. The new boots out of the blanket's end need no line now: he said it
himself.
- **If she says "Not tonight",** she watches from the wall: his lantern going
  down the blue road, and at the ford one blue light going out.

**The cost that stays.** The forge is shut to her for a day after the burial
(the editor's note). The next morning the ford road is dark: every iron is
down, laid in the road with its cage open. His hands are bound. When the
hammer comes back it is slower, one-handed, with a beat missing. The town's
clock is wrong now, and the town says so ("You can hear the river from the
square").

**The lie route costs more, not less.** If she said "I passed nobody", then
on one of Act 1's last mornings Wat's grey mare comes up the south road
alone, with Nell's bundle on the saddle. Brannoc unties it at the gate, with
the survivor there. He sees the iron at her belt. "That's mine. ...You passed
nobody." He goes down the road alone at dusk; from the wall she sees the
light at the ford go out. At dawn he carries his daughter past her without a
look, and never works for her again. The town buries Nell on every route
now, so C08 plays for every player.

**Act 2 and Act 3 do not press the wound again.** Act 2: the hooded buyer
comes for the last two irons, which he beat flat, and he says "Tell her no."
(he knows whose coin); he stands at the war at the gate with his hammer in
his left hand. Act 3: he makes the heart's cage one-handed; on the stair he
hears "Da" once, and looks at the survivor's hands, and picks up the cage.
One grace, if the owner wants it: in Act 2 the memory swap gives her one of
Nell's habits, and at the forge she taps the anvil twice before he strikes,
as only Nell did. He stops. It is a gift, not a wound.

## 6. Everyone else: what they want, hide, and pay

- **Vonnra.** Unchanged at heart: the administrator who did not look. Her

  after the ending he sits on his bench and asks the survivor, "Is it

  ages between one breath and the next: "Oh! Oh, my *knees*!" Both at once.
