# Notes 01: the treatment and the six sample scenes

From the story editor to the writer's successor, and to the owner through the
main session, 5 October 2026. These notes cover `TREATMENT.md`,
`SAMPLE_SCENES.md` and `EMOTIONAL_PASS.md` at `63b97e39`, read against the
letter (`EDITORIAL_LETTER.md`).

**The owner, in his words:**
- "a powerful epic story with real emotional happenings. people being
  devastated, elated, or both. shocks and twists, stuff we figured out that
  was obvious."
- "when maeca kills halloway it should be a HOLY SHIT WHAT THE FUCK kinda
  thing."
- On the old story: "the barefoot thing is a little silly", and "thats a dumb
  reason that people died ... they didn't get shoes? - it needs to be
  something more that a great man made a poor judgement call."

## The verdict

This is the treatment the brief asked for, and it answers all four of the
letter's root causes:
- the setups now hide in kindness and pride;
- every act has a devastation on the trunk;
- Nell is pressed once, at full force;
- and a man the player has laughed with dies in front of them.

The rope (§4.5) is the best-built beat in any of the game's plans. Three
lines in the samples already make a player stop and swallow:
- "She'd have thought I'd come for her."
- "Lid down. Bar across. Sat on it. ... Three days, they knocked."
- "You'd have sat down in the road and waited for me."

The treatment's best idea is the way twists B and A lock together. In the
prologue the narrator goes silent at the water's edge because it is her own
body the player is cutting down, and she says nothing so that her child won't
stop. That re-reads the game's first ten minutes twice, both times through the
player's own hands.

It is not done yet. The notes below are ranked by what each problem costs the
player's feeling. Items 1 and 2 protect the owner's two named demands, so do
them first.

## 1. Protect the HOLY SHIT

The cut itself is right: one stroke, no speech before it, at the moment of his
redemption, and then "Ninety-two." What threatens it is everything around it.

**1a. Cut the tell in the confession.** "Did anyone come out from under it?"
is a question the game writes for the player, and it points at a survivor.
The only Ashford survivor the player knows is Maeca ("The cave mouths."). A
careful player sees that option and starts watching her. It is the loudest
plant in the treatment. Replace it with a question that feeds the decoy
instead: "Does anyone else know?" lets him name Pell, who found the lid barred,
so the eye goes to red.

**1b. Widen the window between the confession and the kill.** This was the
writer's own worry, and it is right. Today the order is the confession, then
the fortune, then the rope the next night. That is too tight, in two ways:
- **There is no room for the false belief that would make the cut a twist.**
  The letter (§3.6, test 4) asked for a moment of peace between them that the
  player thinks they helped make. As things stand, the player believes only
  "she's his friend". It should be "she knows, she has forgiven him, and I was
  there."
- **It stacks Act 1's last two days:** C07, C14, C08, the dark road, the
  confession and the fortune, and then C20 the next night. None of them has
  room to breathe.

**Direction:**
- **Move the confession to the middle of Act 1**, after "She's in my count"
  and before Jory comes home. The player loves him, then learns what he did,
  then goes on loving him. The "Three in!" morning lands harder on a man whose
  secret you know.
- **Put "tell Maeca" on the trunk.** If the player doesn't go to her, she
  comes to them ("He talked to you."). Either way she says "...Did he.", and
  the same night the player watches her walk him home, take his cup and fill
  it. The player reads forgiveness. On the re-read, she has known for ten
  years and has just watched the player tell her.
- **Make the decoy louder that same night** (the Kerchief's bark about the
  rope with the captain's name on it), so the eye moves off her.

**1c. Put the player's hands on the rope.** This is a game, so use what film
can't do. In the treatment he climbs a fixed rope while the survivor watches.
Instead:
- The survivor and Maeca haul him up together. It is the windlass at Ashford,
  turned round.
- It is a held input with no fail state, with rumble on the strain and his
  face rising into the light.
- "Count's right." The haul is finished, and the player has succeeded.
- Then the post end is cut, and the rope runs out through their hands.

Because the input is finished before the cut, nobody can read the cut as "I
failed the button".

**1d. Stage it so that nobody asks "why didn't I stop her?" or "why didn't he
grab the edge?"**
- **His position:** he is a body-length below the lip when the rope goes, not
  at it. His face is lit, but he is out of reach.
- **Hers:** the survivor is on the rope (1c) or at the lip, never beside the
  knife.
- **The music:** let the lift of the rescue play under the climb, and cut it
  dead with the rope. The silence after is louder for the music before it.
- **The light:** light his face from above, by the lamps at the lip, as he
  looks up. That is the shaft-head reversed: ten years ago she came up into
  his lamp and saw his face. Players won't see it at the time, but they will
  the moment they read "91 up."
- **The eye:** put a red kerchief at the lip in the beat before, someone who
  came to help or to watch. The player has heard about "a rope with the
  captain's name on it". Let them look at red while the knife moves.

**1e. Make "Ninety-two" instant.** The number only clicks if "ninety-one" is
already in the player's ear:
- have it said three times before C20, at least once outside the confession
  (a drunk bark at the gate: "Ninety-one up.");
- and at the farm, let him count them up the rope out loud, as he did at
  Ashford, so the player hears his counting voice minutes before Maeca
  finishes the count.

**1f. Continuity: his first laugh.** These three disagree:
- §4.5 makes the laugh in the hole "the first time the player has ever heard
  him laugh";
- the confession has "(A laugh, with nothing in it.)";
- and §1's table has him "on the gate laughing" when Jory comes home.

Keep the first real laugh for the hole. Jory's morning can be the nearest he
comes to it.

## 2. The owner's boots note, and Holloway's call

**No death now turns on footwear.** I checked each one:
- **Ashford:** the lid, not boots. Nobody held anything barefoot.
- **Nell:** the ember-lit irons and the Warden drowned her. Her new boots are
  irony, not the cause.
- **The mother:** the water.
- **Maeca's ruined feet:** three days on wet stone. That is a wound, not a
  death, and the romance keeps "Nobody's held those."
- **Gone:** Pell's letter about boots "signed for full", and "Barefoot"
  everywhere (§8 lists every instance).

**Holloway's act passes the letter's test: it is a great man's terrible,
defensible call.** The confession sells it: a breath to decide, ninety-one up,
the dead climbing close under the last of the living, four hundred asleep
behind him. "Both numbers are right." A player would have dropped the lid.

**One question for the owner.** His words were "a great man made a poor
judgement call". The treatment's call is the right one, at an unbearable
price, but "poor" means wrong. If the owner wants a wrong call, the treatment
has planted it twice already and never used it: he counted "with the lamp in
his eyes".

**Option (b), the lamp in his eyes:**
- What he saw climbing under the last of the living weren't the dead, not
  yet. They were the living from the lower workings, grey with slurry, too far
  gone to call out, climbing slowly. He couldn't see.
- The lid made the dead he thought he saw. Three days in the dark near ember,
  they died and got up, and Maeca finished them one by one before they could.
- She knows this, and he never will. After the kill she says it once, to the
  survivor, in one line.
- "Both numbers are right" turns over: one of them was wrong.

**Weighing it:**
- **For:** it is the owner's own word. It costs one line, and the line is
  already planted. It gives the knife a second turn after the HOLY SHIT. And
  the player who told him "You saved the upper town" learns they comforted him
  over a mistake.
- **Against:** it makes Maeca's act more just, and justice blunts shock. It
  doesn't blunt this one, because the player only learns it after the cut.

I recommend (b). It is the owner's choice, so add it to §10.

**Boot details left over, and the one that reads silly.** Holloway still
carries footwear:
- "the man who knew every soldier's boot size";
- the roll: "Abbot, nines. Ancell, tens, wide." and "Garrison. Boot sizes.";
- "I counted boots for them, once. Every pair.";
- and the daybook's names, listed "with boot sizes".

None of these is a cause any more, and a quartermaster remembering sizes is a
real, specific detail. But the owner has called the boots silly twice, and
this is the old confession line kept on purpose: he will read it as the boots
coming back. It also puts boots in two arcs again, Nell's and Holloway's,
which the bible itself rejected ("two boot reveals in one act blur").

**Cut boots from Holloway entirely.** Give the roll something else a
quartermaster knew, which is also what the confession is about: who each man
left behind. "Abbot. Two bairns. Ancell. His mam. Bede. Nobody." He is
counting the bairns on both sides of the lid. When he says "Both numbers are
right", the player has already watched him reach it. The daybook lists the
names and who they left.

**Keep these:**
- Nell's new boots at the blanket's end: the one boot image left, a child's,
  and irony rather than a cause;
- Maeca's feet, in the romance only;
- Redcowl's "Boots on!", which is an idiom, and funny.

## 3. The woman with the lamp: name the false belief, and give the survivor her purpose back

**The false belief isn't named yet.** The treatment's false belief, "the
drowned in the prologue were strangers", is one no player holds with any
confidence: to them, the drowned are enemies. As written this is a reveal with
a knife in it, not yet a twist (test 1).

**It also exposes a hole the game already has.** The survivor came home
because her mother was failing, and then never looks for her, and nobody in
Act 1 tells the player what became of her. Players will ask "why don't I go
and see my mother?" long before they ask anything about the ford.

**One trunk beat on day 1 fixes both.** She asks after her mother, and someone
she trusts gives her a kind lie. That should be Rook. She wrote the letter,
sold the date, and watched the lamp go down to the water every evening, so she
has the most to hide. "She went in her sleep, pet." That gives the player:
- **a confident, specific false belief:** Mam died in her bed, and I came too
  late;
- **a grave to stand at in Act 1,** with the narrator describing her own
  marker, plainly (that re-reads under twist A);
- **a face for the grief** before Act 2;
- **a double payoff at C32:** Rook lied, and the blow was the player's own.

The details of the lie (when, and how) can carry one fair contradiction for
the player who looks. For example, the back room has been free a week, but the
earth on the grave is fresh.

**The readable ledger is too loud.** At the end of Act 1, line twenty-six
reads "A woman with a lamp. North bank. Seventh evening." The same scene has
just told the player that their prologue hid a catastrophe, so they will go
back to the prologue in their heads, and there a drowned woman lifted a lamp
and did not strike. Many will have the twist before Act 2 opens.

Make line twenty-six readable and innocent: in Vonnra's register, and
pointing somewhere else. For example, "Rook's lodger." The player doesn't know
the mother lodged at the Last Lamp. On the re-read, it is the back room,
"free a week".

**Thin the Act 1 plants:**
- **Keep:** the dropped lamp at C04 and "Back room's free. Has been a week."
  Both are perfect.
- **Cut:** Rook turning toward the marker at C08. It points.
- **Move to Act 2, or cut:** "Nobody's claimed her" in day 2's report. Beside
  a missing mother, it is nearly a tell.

**Rook's guilt needs a cause.** "I told Vonnra you'd be on the road. She paid
me double." What did that buy? If nothing changed because of it, her knife is
a feeling, not a deed. Decide what Vonnra did with the date (say, kept the
lamps burning that week for the traveller she was told to expect), so that
Rook's sale is one link in what drowned them both.

**Plant the ring.** "Paid in a ring" arrives cold. Let the player see the ring
in Act 1, on Rook's hand or on the nail behind the bar. In C32 she puts it on
the table. A thing in the hand beats a fact in the ear.

## 4. The voice: guessability and the recast narrator

This was the writer's worry. The false belief here is the strongest in the
treatment, because nobody asks who a narrator is. Two things will make
players ask.

**The casting brief is a giveaway.** "Low, warm and unhurried, a winter's
tale told to a child" says "mother" in the game's first line.
- Cast the voice plain instead: level and dry, a woman who has told people bad
  news before.
- Let the warmth in only once, at "There you are."
- Give her the valley's accent, lightly, not RP. `VOICES.md` has the narrator
  as RP, but the mother lived here and says "Mam" and "Wick", so the accent is
  right on the re-read.

**Her silence in Rook's kitchen is the loudest plant.** The narrator, who has
never once failed to speak, goes quiet exactly as the player learns of her
death. Attentive players will have it at C32 and spend Act 3 waiting. I would
keep the silence: it is earned, and "stuff we figured out" is part of what the
owner asked for.

But then C43 can't only confirm. It needs a second turn the guesser couldn't
have seen. The treatment names it already, and the samples drop it: the cost.
Every night's ember crowded her out ("It always takes the names first.").
- **Make that audible across the game.** From Act 2's turn, the narrator says
  less: shorter dawns, a line missing here and there. The player hears her
  thinning and takes it for style.
- **At the stair, the player learns why the voice got quieter:** they did it,
  night by night, with their own hands.

That is the knife for the player who guessed, and it passes the cost test (5)
in full.

**Make her leave, not arrive.** In the sample her voice comes "from below"
from its first word. The beat is the voice moving. "Lamp's lit, Wick." should
start where the narrator has always been and then go down into the pale thing,
so that the player hears her leave them. (As written, "I came up with you"
contradicts where the sound is.)

**For the owner's choice: A works without B,** but its best re-read (her
silence at her own body) needs B. Take them together.

**Read "Wick" cold.** It is lovely, and true dialect: "wick" means alive, and
it is also the thread a lamp burns on. Two checks:
- **Plant the meaning on the trunk, early.** No line in the data uses the
  word yet: Rook's "Square's wick with Watchmen" is new.
- **Read "Lamp's lit, Wick" aloud to someone who hasn't seen the treatment.**
  A fair number of players will hear John Wick first. If your listener
  laughs, it isn't the name.

## 5. "You did this": the weakest twist

Strengthen it, or it costs the others.
- **The false belief is thin.** It rests on a tutorial victory, and players
  don't take pride in tutorials. Build the pride for them, on the trunk: three
  people thank her for opening the ford in Act 1, and one of them is Holloway.
  Every thanks is a plant.
- **Rhyme it with Holloway.** She did stop the drowning, and she also broke
  the chain. Let Vonnra say both: "You stopped the drowning. You also did
  this." The player has just heard a man they love say "Both numbers are
  right." In one line the game's two halves lock together.
- **Land the cost after the rope.** §1 says the hole that kills Holloway is
  the one her victory opened, but no scene lets the player see it. That is the
  letter's third test for a loss: the player could have done otherwise, and
  sees it afterwards. Give it to Tam at the lip, the boy who is always right,
  after "Ninety-two", said to nobody: "It started the night the ford went
  dark."
- **It teaches the player to distrust the prologue,** which is where twists B
  and A hide. Keep it about the Warden alone, and never show the drowned or
  the water's edge at C09 (see item 3 on the ledger).
- **The nod as the game's first choice is good.** The silent variant's line is
  crueller than the nod's. Keep both.

## 6. Act 2: one blow too many?

This was the writer's worry. Act 2 doesn't have too many blows. It has one
that comes too soon, and its laugh is in the wrong place.
- **C20b, Holloway at his gate: keep the image, but move it off the day of the
  kill.**
  - On the same dusk it tramples the silence the kill needs, and it turns into
    a horror jump-scare.
  - Give the town two or three nights first: the gate barred, his stool empty,
    nobody counting.
  - The Ashford rhyme is there to take: three days of knocking, which the
    player has heard about from him.
  - On the fourth dusk he is at the gate, and "Somebody's still out.
    ...Always somebody still out." pays off.
- **The laugh comes before the dark, not inside it.** Tam's Pa is the lift
  before the drop. The letter's test is a laugh inside the grief, like Chid's
  flat singing at Nell's grave. Holloway's table is the place:
  - his want was a dog ("Don't tell the men about the dog.");
  - so in the daybook, after the names, put a page of dog names, crossed out
    one by one.
  - The player laughs and breaks at once.
- **His "No." to Sallow should overturn a belief.** Make the player suspect
  him in Act 1: the letter turned face down, a Sallow man seen leaving the
  Watch-house, Holloway evasive. Then the "No." is a reversal, not a fact:
  dead, he protected the person who suspected him.
- **"Sorry." should land on Maeca.** Taking the book to her is the scene, so
  make it the route the game leans toward. She reads the names below the lid,
  and hers isn't among them, because he thought she held the hillside. He
  never counted her.
- **Count the twists.** Ninety-two, Harlan, what you are and the woman with
  the lamp all fall in one act. Keep Harlan and "what you are" small (they
  are a betrayal and an irony), so the two big ones stand alone.

## 7. Brannoc: the beats crowd each other

This was the writer's worry.
- **C07 does grief and guilt at once, and C14 repeats the knowing.** Reorder
  C07 so the mercy comes before the knife:
  1. "Mine. Nell."
  2. "Was it quick?", the answer, and "Thank you".
  3. Only then do his eyes go to the iron at her belt. He has just been given
     the one mercy, and he sees his own work.
  4. End on "She'd have thought I'd come for her.", then silence, then "Forge
     is shut."

  As written, "Was it quick?" comes after the worst line, which makes the
  player's mercy feel small.
- **In C14, cut "reads his own bracket".** He already knows. Keep the one
  action: his hand round the light.
- **Either join C07 and C14 or space them out.** C07 by day and C14 that dusk
  are two full devastations a few hours apart. Run them as one sequence (the
  forge at dusk, "Forge is shut", then he takes his lantern: "You know the
  place."), or put a night between them. One sequence is stronger.
- **There are three iron images in a day:** the thumb on the mark, the hand
  round the light, and every iron laid in the road with its cage open. Cut the
  third. Keep the road dark and the hammer slow: the hammer is the cost the
  player feels.
- **The pride plants are right.** There are three, each a different facet
  (the craft, the boots, the promise). "Nothing on that road you can't see."
  is the cruellest line in the treatment. Keep it.
- **The anvil-tap grace in Act 2** is beautiful, but it is a fourth touch of
  Nell. Keep it only if it stays a grace.

## 8. Smaller things

- **"...Ten." needs the Watch's number planted.** One line in Act 1 does it:
  "Eleven of us and the cat. I count the cat."
- **Tam's Pa needs a plant.** The player has barely met him. Give him one
  sweary line in Act 1, so that the laugh in the hole is a callback.
- **The half at the cave mouths: did they all die?** If they did, Holloway
  would wonder how Maeca lived, and "with the others" in the sample implies
  they did. Say that some of them lived.
- **The Warden's lamp "went into the water",** yet the survivor carries it at
  her belt. Show her taking it out of the shallows. It's a good image: the
  thing he held for two hundred years.
- **Decide what "what she is" is for.** "From the ford. Got up." on the
  readable ledger says the thing `vonnra.f_self` was cut for saying. If it is
  irony (the treatment treats it so, at C30), keep both lines. If it is a
  twist, keep neither.
- **The coat.** On day 1 he gives the survivor his only coat; at the war,
  Maeca wears his coat. There is one coat: decide who has it, and when it
  changes hands.
- **Redcowl's "...Oh, Mam."** in Act 2 sits too close to the summit's "Mam?".
  Give Redcowl another word.
- **Make the loss of "Wick" checkable.** Chid's "Write the names down"
  (`chid.names`) can do it. The survivor writes the word in her journal on day
  3. Later the dawn says "One short word. It was yours.", and there it is in
  her own hand, meaning nothing to her.

## 9. Line notes on the sample scenes

### 1. The first dawn
It lands. Restraint is right here, because this is a plant. Keep "She drinks
the river."
- The performance note is right. Add the casting point from item 4: plain,
  not warm.

### 2. The first night's return
This holds the best plant in the treatment: the loaded crossbow over the
sleeping man, read as care. "That's your last." is a threat that sounds like
care. Keep it exactly as it is.
- **"Gate was open. Somebody had to stand in it."** He was asleep on a stool,
  so this is either a joke or an error. Make it a joke on purpose: give
  Maeca a look.
- **"Don't. I'll want paying."** This is the house rhythm the letter flagged
  (a dry line, then a kicker). His sober voice is prices, so let him name one.

### 3. Brannoc
"She'd have thought I'd come for her." is the best line in the treatment.
- **Reorder the scene** as item 7 describes.
- **Use branch labels.** By the document's own convention, "(quick)" and
  "(not quick)" read as directions for how the lines are said.
- **Use the grey bar once.** "The bar on the anvil goes from orange to grey"
  is C07's existing closing image, so save it for the very end.

### 4. Holloway's confession
"Lid down. Bar across. Sat on it." is the hardest-hitting run in the samples.
The ledger syntax works. "Both numbers are right. That's the trouble with
counting." breaks it, and it should: it is the truest thing he says.
- **Cut** "Did anyone come out from under it?" (item 1a).
- **"(A laugh, with nothing in it.)":** make it a breath out instead (item
  1f).
- **One vessel:** he "looks at the bottle", then "finds the cup's empty".
- **"Wrote them down at the cave mouths. With the others.":** make clear that
  not everyone on the hill died (item 8).
- **Keep the last line,** "Somebody's still out. ...Always somebody still
  out." It sets up C20b.

### 5. The rope
The structure is right: the laugh, the grin, one stroke, the lamp going down
small and out, "Ninety-two.", and only then the choices.
- **"Ha!" isn't a laugh.** Write it as a direction: a real laugh, helpless,
  longer than he means it to be.
- **Staging and the player's hands:** see items 1c and 1d.
- **The non-lover routes need writing.** "Maeca." has a line only for the
  lover. Write what she says to everyone else (if it's nothing, say so), and
  what "(Take hold of her.)" does: she lets you, or she doesn't.
- **Keep "You were up."** It is the most frightening kindness in the game.

### 6. The bottom of the stair
"You'd have sat down in the road and waited for me." is the line the game
exists for. "There you are" is right: it is the lamp lifted to see a face, at
last.
- **It only confirms.** Add the cost (item 4).
- **Move the voice** (item 4).
- **Make the inversion deliberate.** "Stay where it reaches" is held back
  here, and the endings' "Go on" turns it inside out. Make sure that is on
  purpose, and that players can hear it.

## 10. On the owner's choices (§10)

1. **The twists:**
   - "Ninety-two": yes. It is essential.
   - The woman with the lamp and the voice: yes, taken together.
   - "You did this": yes, if item 5 is done. It is what makes the rope the
     player's fault.
2. **The narrator recast:** yes, cast plain (item 4).
3. **The love scenes as unvoiced text:** yes. It is the cheapest plant in the
   game, and it heads off a "Mum was watching?" laugh at the summit.
4. **Holloway at his gate:** keep it, on the fourth dusk (item 6).
5. **The nod as a choice:** yes.
6. **The lamp-iron as her night lantern:** yes. It is what makes Brannoc's
   knowing unmissable.
7. **The lie costs Brannoc's trade:** yes.
8. **"Wick":** yes, after a cold read, and rename the lamplings.
9. **New:** Holloway's call, either right but terrible (as written) or wrong
   but defensible (item 2). I recommend wrong.
