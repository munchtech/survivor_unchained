# Editorial letter: the story against the owner's brief

To the story lead, and to the owner. From the story editor, 5 October 2026.

I have read the whole of Act 1 as a player meets it: the prologue's lines,
C01 to C14, all thirty conversations in `godot/data/content/dialogue.json`,
the barks, the board, the morning reports, the journal. I have also read the
bible, `WRITING_PASS.md`, the Act 2 and Act 3 outlines and the October
editorial study in `docs/editorial/`. This letter judges the whole story,
not only Act 1's beats, because the owner widened the brief.

## The brief, in the owner's words

- "we need better writing", "we need emotional power"
- "a mind breaking twist dosn't exist at the moment, and gut wrenching stuff dosn't hit too hard"
- "we are pulling punches on really hitting things. twists should either twist a knife in or just make you feel gidd with a what the heck just happened. we don't really have either."
- "we really want a powerful epic story with real emotional happenings. people being devastated, elated, or both. shocks and twists, stuff we figured out that was obvious. the whole thing. its all very simple right now."
- Maeca is just Maeca, with a real failing to hold against Holloway: "The barefoot thing is a little silly."
- Holloway "was a good man", whose act got them all killed; now a drunk trying to forget, who still protects, "because he is a GOOD man".
- Brannoc made the thing that got his daughter killed, and it "needs to hit HARD. so hard."
- "when maeca kills halloway it should be a HOLY SHIT WHAT THE FUCK kinda thing."

**The bar I will hold every draft to:** a beat is not done until it would
make a player stop, swallow, or laugh out loud. "Good", "well made" and
"true to the voice" are not the bar. I will say plainly when a beat is not
there yet.

---

## 1. The verdict

The sentences are good. Often they are very good. Nothing in this letter
asks for prettier prose, and most of the cast should keep their voices. The
trouble is the architecture of feeling, and it has four parts. Fix these and
the prose you already have will start to hit.

1. **The setups confess and the payoffs whisper.** Secrets are said almost
   outright on first asking, so the player solves them in the first hour.
   Then the payoff is staged so quietly that a player can miss it. That is
   exactly backwards.
2. **The trunk is hollow.** Every devastating beat sits on a branch. A
   player who makes the kind choices can finish Act 1 without seeing one
   death, one burial or one last word.
3. **One wound is pressed three times.** Each act's major turn is Nell, and
   by the bible's own rule Act 1's major turn is "None".
4. **All the dying happened before the player arrived.** Grief in this game
   is something you dig up, not something you suffer. Nobody the player has
   laughed with is lost in front of them.

That is why it reads as "very simple". The story is not simple: the bible
is deep, layered and consistent. But the player only feels what the game
stages, and the game stages the depth as footnotes.

---

## 2. What to keep, and why

Protect these. They show the cast can carry the weight once the structure
gives it to them.

- **The romances are the best writing in the game.** Take Sella's bolt: "It
  sticks halfway; nobody has ever shot it... it goes home with a sound like
  the last coin put down on a counter." Or Maeca's feet: "Your hands are
  colder than my feet... Hold them anyway. ...Nobody's held those." Or
  Keegan's hand on the wall, which she calls "epizeuxis". These scenes
  already make a player swallow. They are also the game's sharpest
  instrument for devastation, and nothing yet uses them for it (sections 3.4
  and 3.6).
- **C07, the hammer stopping.** It is the best single scene in the game:
  the phrases between strokes, "Mine. Nell.", the iron going grey on the
  anvil. Keep every word.
- **C14's images.** A father walks to his daughter's body down a road lit by
  his own work, and the new boots stick out of the blanket's end.
  Structurally this is the heart of what the owner asked for. It just isn't
  played yet (section 3.5).
- **Comedy that lands.** Snib ("Snib does not know who. Snib knows exactly
  who."), Keegan's handbook, Chid's bellows ("That was a bad day."), and
  Sella throughout. These are laugh-out-loud lines, and they keep the dark
  from going grey.
- **Ember is the dead.** It is the one twist in the plan that truly
  re-reads the whole game, and it is built from the player's own hands.
- **Vonnra's sight is a roof and a long memory.** C09's eyeline rule is a
  clever, fair piece of staging.
- **Redcowl's camp.** "Them first." The child lowers the bowl. This is
  showing at its best.

## 3. The problems, ranked by what they cost the player's feeling

### 3.1 The setups confess; the payoffs whisper

This is the root of "pulling punches", and of "no mind-breaking twist", and
it is the biggest single cost.

**The confessions.** Here is where each big secret is all but said in Act 1,
mostly on first asking:

| Secret (the act that "reveals" it) | Where Act 1 says it |
|---|---|
| Holloway signed for the boots (Act 2) | `holloway.ashford`: "I counted boots for the Ashford garrison, once, and I was good at it." With `maeca.first` ("Barefoot, before you ask."), `maeca.barefoot` ("Boots were signed for. They never came.") and `pell.t_pell` ("somebody had signed for them full"). The player has solved it by the end of day one. |
| Harlan armed the Dig (Act 2) | `harlan.knew`: "He didn't know. ...He didn't know. I did." The first time anyone asks. |
| What you are (Act 2's turn) | `vonnra.f_self`: "You have already died on this road." With `vonnra.ford` ("another one coming on"), `chid.before` ("You were cold when they brought you in"), the prints and the respawn. |
| Vonnra lit the lamps (Act 3) | The player assembles it in Act 1 by design, and the reward is that she says their name. |
| Chid is old, and "C." (Act 3) | "Nobody's made a C like that in... well. Ages."; "Chid's not aged a day since my mam was a girl."; "Morning comes. It always has. I should know." |
| Redcowl is Rav's brother (Act 1 or 2) | "Redcowl owes Rav a leg."; "A little bird... sews a fair seam." |

None of these can twist, because the player knew. A twist needs the player
to believe something false with confidence, and nothing in the game builds a
false belief. The narrator never lies, which is a good rule, and every
evasion points straight at its own secret.

**The whispers.** Now look at how the payoffs are staged. "Unremarked",
"never framed for", "nobody says" and "does the rest" turn up 24 times in
the cinematic scripts and the bible:

- C27, the boots (Act 2's major turn): "Nobody says 'you killed her' or 'it
  was the boots'; the player who has seen the nail does the rest." That nail
  was in the background of C07, "never mentioned, never framed for", a
  dozen hours earlier.
- C43, the game's summit: "Nobody says 'you burned her'. The player says it."
- C08, Vonnra's alto: "mixed so that a listener finds her rather than hears
  her."
- C09, the act's stinger: the ledger is "too small to read".
- C08, the mother's grave: "she never turns to it."
- C14, Brannoc and his irons: "On the road he never looks up at the irons.
  That is the whole performance of shot 6." That whole performance is an
  absence.

Restraint multiplies a feeling the scene has already built. It cannot
create one. Silence after a blow is devastating; silence instead of a blow
is just silence. The bible's first rule ("never let a character say an
answer out loud before the act that answers it") has turned into a habit of
never letting the payoff land out loud either.

**The fix is to invert it.**

- **Hide the setup in plain sight.** A plant should read as something else
  the first time: a joke, a kindness, small talk, a different mystery. It
  should be on screen, unmissable and innocent.
- **Land the payoff in full view.** Frame it, hold it, and if it needs
  saying, say it once and plainly. Then let the silence come after.

Put simply: the answer may be said, but only at the payoff. Use your
restraint on the setups and on the aftermath, never on the blow itself.

**And give every secret one job.** Decide for each whether it is a
**twist**, which the player must not know, or **irony**, which the player
knows and a character does not. Right now most are neither: the player half
knows, and nothing is done with it.

- Chid works best as irony. Let the player know he is Unchained, and use the
  dread of that when the chain's choice comes.
- Harlan works best as a twist. Cut "I did." from `harlan.knew`, so the line
  ends "He didn't know." and he goes back to counting. Let C08's silent
  mouth and `harlan.jory_now` carry the doubt.
- The Holloway and Maeca thread must be a twist (section 3.6).

### 3.2 The trunk is hollow: every devastation is a branch

Take a player who cures the wolves, bargains for the caravan, lies to
Brannoc to spare him and never collects the lamps clues. They see no death,
no burial, no road back, no last words and no accusation. Their Act 1 has no
devastating beat in it at all, and to them the story is "very simple",
correctly.

Here is what each beat needs before it plays:

- C07 needs day 2 or later, Brannoc already met, daytime, and a second
  visit.
- C08 and C14 need the truth told to Brannoc. C14 also needs the player to
  pass the smithy at dusk and choose "I'll show you".
- C10 and C11 need the violent routes.
- The accusation needs `LAMPS_CTX`.
- The name on the mother's marker needs the player to have named her at
  creation.
- Maeca killing Holloway (C23) is one variant of three.

Branching is a strength, but the trunk has to carry the weight. **Every
player should meet at least one devastation in each act that they cannot
miss.** Their choices should bend how it lands, not whether it lands.

The lie to Brannoc shows the problem best. Today the lie route is the
cheaper one: a bark, some pedlars stopped at the gate, and the truth
deferred to Act 2. It should cost more. If the player lies, let the truth
reach Brannoc before the act ends and in front of the player (the grey mare
coming up the south road alone, say). Then the lie is the knife.

### 3.3 One wound, pressed three times

The bible's own table, "The extremes, rationed":

| Act | Major |
|---|---|
| 1 | None: Nell, as written |
| 2 | Nell's boots |
| 3 | Ember is the dead: the player burned Nell, and hears her say "Da" |

Each act's great turn is the same twelve-year-old, whom the player never met
alive. The first press hurts; by the third the player is braced, and braced
is numb. Meanwhile the people the player spends hours with get no
devastation of their own: Holloway, Maeca, Rook, Chid, Keegan, Sella, Rav,
the lovers, and the survivor's own mother. And Act 1's major turn is "None"
by policy, with the rule that each one is "said in one line". That is the
punch being pulled in writing.

**What to do.**

- Make Nell Act 1's full-force wound, and land it (section 3.5).
- Give Acts 2 and 3 different wounds, in people the player has loved on
  screen.
- Nell's "Da" at the bottom of the stair can stay, as an echo inside a
  bigger scene, not as its point.
- Retire the rule "each said in one line". Replace it with "each landed in
  full view".

### 3.4 All the dying happened before the player arrived

The dead in this story are Nell, Wat, twenty-four others at the ford,
Corran, Dannet, Ewan, Ashford, the fever year, the survivor's mother,
Jessop, Brother Tobin, Harlan's sister and Pell's sister. Every one of them
is dead before the prologue starts. In Act 1, the only people the player
knew alive who die on screen die because the player killed them
(Greymuzzle, Redcowl, the teamsters if the Roost burns) or neglected them
(Aldo, who is a bark). Act 2's outline keeps it up: C23's killing happens
off screen and the survivor "arrives to his count of boots still open". The
boots are reported by Rook.

The game turns grief into archaeology: the player uncovers losses instead of
suffering them. Devastation needs a person the player has laughed with,
taken out of the world in front of them, with an empty place left behind:
a chair, a shut shop, a bark that stops.

**What to do.** Put at least one such loss in Act 1, or at the very top of
Act 2, and one in each act after that. My tests for whoever the writer
chooses:

1. The player has had at least three warm scenes with them, and laughed in
   one.
2. Their absence changes the town's day.
3. The player could have done something differently, and the game lets them
   see it afterwards.

### 3.5 Brannoc: the core of his arc is never staged

The owner's words: he made the thing that got his daughter killed, and it
must "hit HARD. so hard."

**What is there.**

- C07: he asks after Nell, and the hammer stops.
- C14: the road lit by his irons, Wat, and carrying her home.
- C08: the marker.
- Act 2, C27: Rook tells the survivor about the boots.
- Act 3, C43: he looks at her hands.

**What is missing:** the moment Brannoc *knows* that his irons drowned her,
on screen, with the player watching. Today that knowledge is:

- optional, since it needs the lamps mystery or the lamp-iron shown to him;
- a stage direction, in `brannoc.nell_ditch`: "looks at the two irons still
  on the rack, and then he doesn't look at anything";
- an absence in C14 ("he never looks up");
- kept off screen "for an act" (C14's own notes);
- passed on by Rook in Act 2, "not to him, to the survivor".

The player never watches him understand. That is the scene the owner is
asking for, and it isn't written.

**What it needs,** as directions for the writer to choose between and build
on:

- **Put his iron in his hands.** The player has carried the Warden's
  lamp-iron, with his mark under the socket, since the prologue. Make it
  something they *use*, so their hands are on his work every night. Then
  stage the truth so that he is holding his own mark when he learns how Nell
  died, whatever order the player found things in.
- **Make his pride the knife.** Plant a boast early, as small talk: the
  irons were the best work he's done in years; he lit the ford road; it's
  safe now. The cruellest version is that he told her the road was safe
  *because he had lit it*. The player hears it as a proud man's chatter.
  C07 and C14 turn it round.
- **Bring the boots into Act 1, as irony.** He tells the player plainly,
  with pride, that the irons money bought her first new boots. The player
  knows what the irons did, and he doesn't yet. Then C14's insert ("The
  blanket's end, and two small new boots out of it") needs no line, because
  the plant was said out loud. Restraint works here, because the setup was
  loud. Act 2 then becomes *him* finding out, which is a scene, rather than
  Rook reporting it, which is a footnote.
- **In C14, let him look up once.** He stops under one iron and reads his
  own bracket, and the player watches him understand. What he does next is
  the writer's: puts it out with his bare hand; leaves them all burning and
  carries her through his own light; or something better. It must be one
  action, held, with no line over it.
- **Test:** can a player who took the default choices say afterwards, "he
  found out, and I was there"? Today they cannot.

### 3.6 Holloway and Maeca: not loved, solved on day one, killed off screen

The owner's bar for this beat is the highest in the game, so I give it the
most room.

**Where it stands.**

- **Holloway is respected, not loved.** His best lines are rueful: "I'd give
  a month's pay to hear him hum."; "I owe Corran a hole in the ground, and
  an apology he can't hear." The player never sees him protect anyone at a
  cost to himself. The drinking the owner describes exists in one line of
  Sella's ("Holloway's drinking more than he's paying").
- **Maeca's grievance is in her name** ("Maeca Barefoot", and "Barefoot,
  before you ask") and in her first conversation ("Boots were signed for").
  Holloway confesses his part the first time he's asked. Pell's sister's
  letter says it a third time.
- **The killing is one variant of three, and off screen** (C23).

**The bar for "HOLY SHIT WHAT THE FUCK."** Every one of these has to hold:

1. **The player loves Holloway.** Not respects: loves. That takes three or
   more on-screen acts of protection, each costing him something, with at
   least one while he is drunk and failing and does it anyway. The night
   count ("One in. Count's right, for once.") is the right instinct, but it
   is a bark. Make it a scene: the first time she comes back from a night
   fight, he is at the gate, drunk, with a lamp, waiting. The drinking must
   be grief, never comedy. The player should notice what he drinks to
   forget before they learn what it is.
2. **The act that killed them is a good man's decision.** I'll take the
   writer's proposal, judged by one test: hearing it, could the player have
   made the same call for the same reasons? If the answer is "no, he was
   weak or careless", the beat turns into justice, and justice doesn't
   shock.
3. **Maeca's motive is planted but hidden.**
   - Cut "Barefoot" everywhere (list in section 4).
   - Cut every line in which Maeca shows a grievance against Holloway in
     Act 1. If she has a visible quarrel with him at all, make it a decoy:
     the bounty, which is about wolves.
   - Better still, make her *kind* to him. She walks him home when he's
     drunk; she knows how he takes his drink; she never lets him pay. Later,
     every kindness re-reads as a hunter's patience.
   - Some lines are already halfway there, and should be kept and aimed:
     `maeca.t_maeca`, "Men, for the garrison... Men are easier. They keep to
     paths and they talk while they walk."; and her romance line, "I've lain
     with my ear to a lot of things to see if they'd live."
4. **It happens in front of the player, fast, at the worst possible
   moment.** The moment of most love is the right one: just after
   Holloway's bravest act, or in a moment of peace between him and Maeca
   that the player thinks they helped make. No speech before. One action.
   The camera does not cut away. If anything is said, it comes after, and
   it is short.
5. **It is inevitable afterwards.** A player should be able to list five
   moments they saw and did not read. If they can't, the plants are too
   hidden; if they saw it coming, they are too loud.
6. **It is not optional.** The owner says "when", not "if". Let the
   player's choices change what follows (whether the player stops her, what
   the town does, what becomes of her). Do not let them change whether it
   happens.
7. **If Maeca is the player's lover, it is the same scene, and it costs
   more.** The player's lover kills the player's friend. Do not soften that
   route. It is the strongest version.

### 3.7 No twist builds a false belief, and the best seeds are unused

A mind-breaking twist overturns something the player is sure of. This game
only ever confirms what the player suspects. Here are the tests I will run
on every twist the writer proposes:

1. **The false belief.** What, exactly, is the player sure of until the
   twist? Name it. If the answer is "nothing", it is a reveal, not a twist.
2. **The re-read.** List at least five earlier moments that change meaning.
   Each must have read naturally as something else the first time.
3. **Fair but hidden.** Every plant is on screen, unmissable, and innocent.
   No plant lives only in an optional branch, an unframed prop or a
   high-difficulty mystery.
4. **"It was obvious".** Afterwards, does the player feel it was there all
   along? (The owner's "stuff we figured out that was obvious".)
5. **The cost.** Does it take something the player cares about: a person, a
   deed they were proud of, a belief about themselves?
6. **The player's own hands.** It is best when it is something the player
   *did*, seen again (the cinematics README already says this).

Some seeds already in the ground could take a twist, and none of them is
used yet. They are for the writer to test, not prescriptions:

- **The letter in her coat** (C01): "the ink has run to blue water". It
  calls her home because her mother is failing, but her mother died a week
  before the ford. Who wrote it, and when?
- **The twenty-seventh line** of Vonnra's ledger (C09). When was it
  written, and what is on it?
- **The prologue's victory.** The bible says it outright: "The prologue's
  victory was the chain's first break." The player's first heroic act
  causes the tremors, speeds the Dig and opens the ground under Tam's farm.
  No scene ever lands this on the player. "You did this" with the player's
  proudest moment is the knife-twist the owner describes.
- **The nod** (C03). Her first mercy, to the thing that drowned her. What if
  mercy was the catastrophe?
- **Rook's double pay.** "She's never paid me double for anything."

### 3.8 The acts end in whispers, and nothing swings upward

- **Act 1 ends on a recap.** The fortune runs seven readings of what the
  player did. Its turn is the accusation, answered by her saying their
  name. The stinger is a ledger "too small to read". Of these, only the
  name has any weight, and only for players who accused her. Act 1 needs a
  real turn: something that changes what the player believes, in full
  view. If the ledger is the stinger, let the player read it, and let what
  is written there be the shock. Cut the readings to the three that
  mattered most in that player's game.
- **Act 2 ends on an invitation** ("Come down with me. It is not far. It is
  only deep."). It is a good line, but it is an act ending in a whisper
  after an act of whispers.
- **Elation is missing.** The owner wants players "devastated, elated, or
  both". The small joys are real (Tam's "The water's clear!", Harlan's
  "Alive. Alive!", Chid's "It WORKS."), but there is no great upward swing.
  In this genre the biggest elation is earned loyalty arriving at the worst
  moment. Act 2's war at the gate already holds the roll call for it
  (C31), but as written it is a static "key image" of a line of people.
  Make it a reversal: the line is breaking, and then they come (the Pack,
  Redcowl's forty-one, Keegan with the book inside her breastplate), each
  of them the result of a choice the player made days earlier. That is the
  "both" the owner asks for, especially if someone the player loves falls
  in the same scene.
- **Escalation.** The personal and the cosmic don't lock together until the
  bottom of the stair. Act 2 should be where what the player has done to
  the town and what the player *is* begin to cost the same people.

### 3.9 The mother has no face for the player

Act 3's summit is the mother's voice saying the survivor's name, with
"Mam?" as the choice. It rests on a person the player knows only as an
absence: "not quite where you left it", one question from Chid, a letter
whose ink has run, and a grave nobody looks at. You cannot be devastated by
losing someone you were never given.

**What to do.** Give the player one concrete, specific, slightly odd thing
about her in the prologue: a phrase, a habit, a song, how she said the
name. Each background can have its own. Let the dawns take it piece by
piece, so the player notices what is going. Then let her say *that* at the
bottom of the stair. Without it, "Mam?" is just a word.

### 3.11 Voices that blur

`VOICES.md` is strong, and the leads honour it in the big scenes. But some
templated lines, written against a list rather than a person, have flattened
the middle of the cast.

- **The calling remarks (`say_calling`).**
  - The stalker's: six people make the same observation. Rook: "You came
    in without the door making a sound." Chid: "You came in from the side."
    Vonnra: "You came to my window from the side the light does not reach."
    Keegan: "You have been standing there for some time." Sella: "You came
    up behind me without a sound." Pell: "You've been standing there longer
    than I noticed."
  - Holloway ("You count the ways out when you walk into a room.") and Rav
    ("You counted the doors when you sat down.") say the same thing.
  - The arcanist's: four people tell one joke. Holloway: "set the barracks
    roof alight". Redcowl: "set fire to my tent". Wenna: "set my sage
    alight". Rook: "do it outside".
  - **The fix:** write each from the speaker's trade, as Rav's, Wenna's,
    Brannoc's and Pell's warden line already are. Rewrite only the
    duplicates.
- **The confiding aside, "Don't tell X".** It is Sella's by right
  (discretion is her trade), and she uses it five times. Holloway, Harlan,
  Rook and the Wayfinder also use it, so it no longer marks anyone. Leave it
  with Sella.
- **The house rhythm.** A dry line, then "...", then a rueful kicker. It is
  shared by Holloway, Rook, Rav, Sella, Harlan and Maeca. Brannoc, Keegan,
  Chid, Snib and Vonnra are unmistakable; the middle six converge. As
  Holloway is rebuilt into a drunk, give him a syntax of his own (drink
  loosens grammar before it loosens secrets).
- **Kept on purpose:** Harlan and Jory share "Thank you. I think. I'll know
  when I've stopped feeling sick." `VOICES.md` records it as a family
  echo, and it works. Keep it, and don't let a third person borrow it.

### 3.12 Smaller costs

- **After the hardest beats, the game drops straight back to a shop menu.**
  The day after the burial, Brannoc's hub opens on "(He works...) Steel or
  fur?". The stage direction is lovely, but let the forge be shut to the
  player for a day. That is a cost the player feels in their own hands.
- **The accusation of Vonnra costs nothing in Act 1** (respect +25, trust
  −15, invisible). If it is the act's turn, it should change something the
  player can see by the next morning.
- **Wat is a name on a notice before he is a boss.** That's fine for
  Brannoc's scene, but give the player one living detail of him (the grey
  mare is there; use it earlier) so his rising means something on its own.

## 4. The owner's three people: acceptance tests for the treatment

**Maeca.**
- No "Barefoot" anywhere. These all go:
